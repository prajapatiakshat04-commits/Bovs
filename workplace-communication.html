Workplace Communication — The Skills That Move Careers

Technical ability gets you hired. Communication determines what happens after. This guide covers everyday email and chat, how to give feedback that lands, how to manage upward, how to handle disagreement without damage, and how to work across cultures and time zones.

The principle behind almost everything here: write so the other person knows exactly what you want them to do. Most workplace frustration is not disagreement — it is guessing.

1. Email That Gets Answered

ElementGood practice
Subject lineSpecific and searchable: "Approval needed: revised Q3 budget by Thu 3pm". Never "Question" or "Quick one".
First lineState the ask. "Could you approve the revised budget by Thursday?"
ContextTwo or three lines of why it matters, for someone who has no memory of a conversation you had last month.
DeadlinesGive a real date and time, with the timezone if you work across regions. Say clearly which item is optional.
LengthUnder 200 words unless the topic genuinely needs more. Long emails hide the request.
TonePoliteness costs nothing. Over-apologising weakens your position.

Short and direct is not rude. What reads as rude is vagueness — forcing a colleague to decode what you need and by when.

2. Chat Etiquette

  • Use threads. Answer inside the thread so the context stays with the answer.
  • Do not send a one-word message for something that needs a decision. Use a thread and put the decision in writing.
  • Add people deliberately. Adding the whole channel to a small question is how people learn to ignore notifications.
  • Say when you are done. A closing line stops the follow-up nobody wanted.
  • Expect delay. Chat is not email; nobody is expected to answer in four minutes. Do not read silence as disagreement.
  • Take hard conversations off chat. Anything emotionally loaded belongs in a call.

3. Giving Feedback That Lands

The format that works: observation → impact → request. Describe behaviour you actually observed, explain what it did, then ask what they want to do differently.

WeakStrong
"You need to communicate better.""In Tuesday's meeting the decision about the vendor was made but not shared with the design team, so they redid the work. Can we agree that decisions get posted in the channel after standup?"
"That report was sloppy.""The figures on slide four do not match the spreadsheet, which made the team question the rest of the numbers. Worth a second pass before it goes out?"
"Great job everyone!""You handled the outage calmly and the update every 30 minutes kept everyone calm too. That is exactly what we needed."
  • Soon after, not much later. Feedback about something three weeks old is really feedback about your memory.
  • Privately, for anything corrective.
  • In front of others, when it is praise. Specific praise is what people remember.
  • Ask, do not accuse. "I may have missed context — can you walk me through it?" is disarming and usually reveals a real problem.

4. Receiving Feedback

  1. Do not defend in the first ten seconds. Your instinct will be to explain, and it guarantees the rest goes unheard.
  2. Ask one clarifying question. "Can you give me an example?" often reveals you were both talking past each other.
  3. Separate the person from the point. Feedback on one behaviour is not a verdict on your ability.
  4. Decide what to actually change. You cannot fix everything; pick one or two things and do them visibly.
  5. Come back later. "That helped — I started doing X last week" closes the loop and makes the next conversation easier.

5. Managing Up

Your manager is not a mind-reader, and they are usually deciding between several priorities.

  • Give status before you are asked. A short weekly update removes them from chasing you.
  • Flag problems early. "This will slip by two days, here is why, here is what I need" is valued far more than a surprise.
  • Bring options, not just a problem. Two approaches and a recommendation is more useful than a report of an obstacle.
  • Ask about their priorities. "What are you most worried about this quarter?" tells you what to prioritise when everything is urgent.
  • Disagree once, clearly. Ask for the goal behind the instruction, state your concern with a reason, propose an alternative, then commit. Do not re-litigate it every week.

6. Difficult Conversations & Conflict

SituationWhat to do
Work is being repeatedly reworkedAsk what a "done" looks like in their words. Most rework is a mismatch in expectations that was never written down.
A colleague misses deadlinesRaise it with facts and impact, once, privately. If it continues, take it to your manager or HR — not to the wider team.
Credit is being takenDocument your contribution in writing at the time. In the moment: "Just to be clear, I led the migration piece — worth capturing that for the review." Neutral, factual, on the record.
You made a real mistakeSay it early, say what you will do, and do it. "I sent the wrong version — I have recalled it and double-checked the rest" beats a quiet fix discovered later.
Persistent disagreementCheck whether you disagree on facts or on values. On facts, find out. On values, you may just need to accept it and stop arguing.
Escalate properly. Grieving with your manager first is normal. Going straight to HR for a one-off clash damages your reputation. Use formal channels for harassment, discrimination, safety or repeated serious misconduct — and keep records.

7. Working Across Cultures & Time Zones

  • Learn the indirectness. In some cultures "interesting" means no, and "we will consider it" means no. In others, direct disagreement is how respect is shown.
  • Name your working hours explicitly and include the timezone in signatures and calendar invites.
  • Never be the only person awake. Rotate meeting times across regions so one country always carries the inconvenience.
  • Write things down. When meaning can be lost in tone, the written record protects everyone.
  • Assume competence, adjust style. Cultural differences are about norms, not ability.

8. A Quick Self-Check

Before you send any important message, ask: Could this person tell what I want, by when, and what is optional? Is anything here that a screenshot in six months would make uncomfortable? Have I said what I actually mean? Three questions. Most workplace communication problems disappear.

Communication is a learnable skill. Pick one improvement — subject lines, or one clearer status update a week — and do it for a month. It compounds faster than any course.
