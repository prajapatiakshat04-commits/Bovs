Networking — Relationships That Move Your Career

Most jobs are found through people, not job boards. Networking is not schmoozing and it is not collecting business cards — it is a set of genuine relationships with people who can vouch for you. This guide covers where to find them, how to start conversations without an excuse, what to send afterwards, and how to keep it going without it becoming a chore.

The rule that makes it work: give before you ask. People respond to those who have been useful. The most effective networkers are the ones who recommend others, share articles, congratulate wins and check in with no request attached.

2. Your Network Is Not a List — It Is a Portfolio

Useful framing: your network is the set of people who would vouch for you if asked. That is a portfolio, not a contact list. Build it by making yourself worth vouching for — and by knowing, roughly, what each contact could actually say about you.

Practically, it helps to keep a simple note for each significant contact: what they do, when you last spoke, what you talked about, and what would be genuinely useful to them. Not a spreadsheet of leverage — a memory aid so you are not boring.

3. Starting A Conversation Without An Excuse

Most people stall because they want a reason to contact someone. You do not need a reason; you need a reason specific enough to be interesting.

  • Congratulate genuinely. A new role, a launch, an award, a talk they gave. "Saw your talk on X — the point about Y stuck with me."
  • Share something relevant. An article, a tool, a piece of research that connects to their work.
  • Ask a narrow question. "What does a good first 90 days look like in that team?" is easy to answer. "How is the industry?" is not.
  • Respond to their work. A specific, thoughtful comment beats a generic "great post".
  • Reconnect after a gap. "We overlapped at X two years ago — you moved into Y, how is it going?"
What kills a cold message: "I'm looking for a job in your field — could you help?"
What works: "I saw your team is hiring for X. I have done Y — if that is useful context, happy to share what I learned doing it. No worries either way." Short, gives them something, makes refusal easy, and asks for almost nothing.

4. Following Up Once, Well

  1. Within a week of the conversation.
  2. Reference something specific you actually discussed — proves you listened.
  3. Add one thing of value — the article they asked about, an introduction, the answer to their question.
  4. Under 120 words. One paragraph. No attachments unless asked.
  5. No ask on the first follow-up. The relationship is the point at this stage.

Then leave it alone. The common mistake is sending a second follow-up because there was no reply. There usually is not one, because the person was busy, not because they disliked you. One follow-up is professional; three is pressure.

5. Keeping It Alive Without Effort

  • Set a monthly reminder to message two people with no request attached.
  • Reply to every real message within a day or two, even briefly.
  • Congratulate promotions and new roles — it takes thirty seconds and it is the highest-value message you will send all year.
  • Recommend other people. This is the single most valuable thing you can do for your network, and it costs five minutes.
  • Stay in touch with former teams after you leave. People move, and their new employers are your next opportunity.
  • Give a talk or write something. The fastest way to inbound requests is to have something of yours out there.

7. Networking In Remote And Hybrid Teams

If you work remotely, you have to replace the office's accidental contact with deliberate contact — because the conversations you would have overheard no longer happen to you.

  • Book a recurring coffee with one colleague you have no reason to talk to.
  • Turn on your camera for one-to-ones. It measurably changes the quality of the conversation.
  • Join cross-team channels, even if they are not your department.
  • Go in to the office on the same days as the people you want to know.
  • Ask your manager which teams your work touches, and talk to them directly.

8. When You Feel Bad About It

Networking feels transactional to some people, especially early in a career when relationships feel thin. The reframe that helps: you are not extracting value. You are becoming the kind of person who knows people, shares what they find useful, and tells them about opportunities. That person gets helped back, not as a favour, but because relationships run both ways.

Start small. One message a week to someone who already knows you. Ten weeks in, you will have a network you did not have before.

Track it lightly. Keep a simple list of names, where you met, and when you last spoke. Not for leverage — so that the tenth follow-up does not arrive in the same week as the interview.
