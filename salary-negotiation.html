Salary Negotiation — How To Ask For What You Are Worth

Negotiating is a normal part of being hired, not a favour to request. Most people leave money on the table in one or two short conversations. This guide covers researching the market, choosing your moment, stating a number without flinching, handling a low offer, and negotiating more than base salary.

What is negotiable, in rough order of real value: equity and bonus → sign-on or joining bonus → work arrangement and leave → title → review timeline → base salary. Companies have far more flexibility on the first four than the last one, and people focus on base because it is the only number they feel allowed to say.

1. Research Before You Negotiate

Never negotiate on instinct. Bring evidence.

SourceHow to use it
Published salary surveysReliable ranges for role, industry and region. Multiple sources, because each is wrong in a different direction.
Levels.fyi and equivalentsBest for tech-heavy and public-data roles, especially total compensation.
Job postings with rangesThe company itself publishes these. Ask them to justify the band.
RecruitersUsually know the approved range for the role even when they will not disclose it. Ask what the band is.
Your networkSomeone doing a comparable job nearby. Ask about bands, not exact figures.
Offers in handThe strongest possible data point. See section 5.

Write down three numbers before the call: a target, your acceptable minimum, and your walk-away. Negotiating without these is negotiating without a position.

2. Know Your Position

  • Your leverage is real and specific. A competing offer, a rare skill, a project only you understand, notice period timing, or a deadline on their side.
  • Your leverage is weaker when you are desperate, the market is flooded, the role is easily replaceable, or you are negotiating a first job with no evidence of your market value.
  • Getting an offer does not obligate you to accept it. The only things that do are your visa status, your notice period, and finances. Know which of those apply to you.
  • You can negotiate before an offer too — ask for the salary band at the start of the process, before they fall in love with you. It is easier then, not harder.

3. When To Negotiate

TimingAssessment
Early conversation, before final roundsBest for preventing an offer below your floor. "What range has been budgeted for this role?"
Immediately after the offer is madeFine, but there is more leverage before you sign anything.
After you have acceptedAlmost impossible without a strong reason. Do not accept then ask.
During the annual review cyclePossible, especially with market movement and evidence. See career growth.
When you are counter-offered to leaveCommon but capped. Expect about 10-20%, and consider leaving anyway.

4. How To State Your Number

The formula that works: thanks + rationale + number + close.

"Thank you, I'm genuinely interested. Based on my research for a senior analyst role in this city, and the scope of what you described, I was aiming for around 95,000. Is there flexibility to get closer to that?"
  • Say a number. A range invites them to pick the bottom. If pushed, give a range with the figure you want at the top, clearly labelled as your target.
  • Give the reason immediately before the number. Market data, scope, a qualification they asked for, or a competing offer.
  • Anchor above your target. If you want 95, open at 105-110. Anchoring is normal, not dishonest.
  • Then stop talking. The silence after the number feels unbearable to you and is completely normal to them.
  • Be warm, not apologetic. No "sorry to ask" — you are asking for what the role is worth.
Never bluff about another offer. Offers are checked, always, and a fabricated one ends the process and, in a small industry, follows your name. If you have one, say it plainly and describe the role.

5. Handling A Low Offer

  1. Thank them first. Always. It keeps the relationship intact and signals you are not being emotional.
  2. Ask what the band is. "Is this the approved range for the level?" Some low offers are simply misclassified — the job may be graded higher than you assumed.
  3. Give the gap and the reason. "The research I found for this role and location starts around 90, and the scope includes X, so I had expected something closer to that."
  4. Ask them to close the gap. Not "take it or leave it" — a question. Most people negotiate well; few are negotiating in bad faith.
  5. Get the improvement in writing if you accept a conditional raise, with a date and a person responsible.
  6. Do not resign from a current job to negotiate a new offer unless you already have a signed contract. The offer is not guaranteed until it is signed.

6. Negotiating More Than Base Salary

ItemHow to ask
Sign-on / joining bonusMost flexible item on the list. Often available even when base is fixed.
Equity or optionsAsk for the grant, the vesting schedule, and the strike price. Compare total value to cash, not headline number.
Review timeline"What is the review cadence, and what would trigger an off-cycle increase?" Get an answer with a date attached.
TitleWorth having if it is real and used, not if it exists only to sound impressive on your business cards.
Leave and flexibilityUsually easier to agree than money, and it often affects retention more.
LocationA remote-friendly arrangement or a relocation package can be worth a great deal, especially in high-cost cities.
Budget for trainingAsk what is available for courses, conferences or certifications. A company that says no is telling you something.

7. Common Mistakes

  • Accepting the first offer because it felt good to be chosen. Enthusiasm is not compensation.
  • Negotiating by email in long messages. A call, or a short written message followed by a call, works better.
  • Comparing yourself to the wrong peer. You are paid for the value of the role, not your seniority.
  • Apologising for wanting more. It weakens every number that follows.
  • Negotiating after signing. Too late.
  • Taking the first counter without thinking. See section 8.
  • Ignoring equity when comparing offers. Compare total compensation over three years, not the headline base.

8. Counter-Offers And Staying

A counter-offer is usually a sign that your employer knows you are about to leave. It can be a genuinely good outcome, but weigh these things first:

  • Counter-offers are capped — usually in the 10-20% range, and often without the title or scope you actually wanted.
  • They come with a clock. You have just told them you were looking, which changes how the relationship is viewed.
  • If your reason for leaving was the manager, a raise fixes money, not the cause.
  • Some employers will treat you differently after asking to be let go. Not always, but it happens.

Either way, keep the outside process alive until you have signed something. Not as leverage — because a signed offer is the only thing that ends the uncertainty.

One conversation, once. Most people negotiate badly because they negotiate many times — in the interview, then with the recruiter, then with the manager, each time to a different person. Pick one person, make your best case once, and let the answer stand.
