Job Interviews — Prepare, Perform, Then Negotiate

Most interviews are decided on three things: whether you can do the work, whether you will be easy to work with, and how much you want it. Preparation moves all three. This guide covers company research, the STAR method, the questions that catch people out, how to read the room on format, and what to do in the days after.

Prepare stories, not speeches. Before any interview, write out six real examples from your history — one for a challenge, one for a failure, one for a conflict, one for a deadline, one for leadership, one for a quick decision you got wrong. Rehearse them once out loud. In the interview you will not invent an answer, you will retell one.

1. Research That Pays Off

Before the interview, know these six things. Ninety minutes of research is enough.

  • What they sell, and to whom. Be able to describe the business in two sentences, as if explaining it to a friend.
  • Who the competitors are. It shows you thought about their position, not just your own.
  • What is happening right now. A funding round, a product launch, an expansion, a new leader — these usually shape the actual problems of the role.
  • Who you would meet. Look up the interviewer on LinkedIn. Knowing their background turns an interrogation into a conversation.
  • The job description, word by word. Underline the top three responsibilities. Most questions will come from them.
  • Why the role is open. Growth, replacement, or a new function? It changes what they worry about.

Also prepare two questions for them. Not about the organisation — they wrote that. About the friction you noticed: "You are hiring three engineers this quarter. What part of the roadmap is hardest to staff?"

2. The STAR Method For Behavioural Questions

"Tell me about a time when..." is not a request for a story, it is a request for evidence in four parts.

PartWhat you sayTime
SituationThe context, in one or two lines. Name the constraint — deadline, budget, headcount, conflict.~20%
TaskWhat specifically you were responsible for. Use "I", not "we".~15%
ActionWhat you did, and why. This is the part candidates rush — spend most of your time here.~45%
ResultWhat changed. A number if you have one. What you learned, briefly.~20%
The most common failure is team credit. "We migrated the database" tells the interviewer nothing about you. "I led the migration, chose the phased rollout over the big-bang approach, and wrote the rollback plan we used twice" tells them exactly what you are capable of.

3. The Questions That Catch People Out

QuestionWhat they are testingHow to answer well
Tell me about yourselfWhether you can summarise your own valuePresent to relevant → past → why this role. Ninety seconds, ending on why you want this job.
What is your greatest weakness?Self-awareness and whether you can fix thingsA real weakness plus the concrete change you made about it. Not "I work too hard".
Tell me about a failureHonesty and resiliencePick a genuine miss, own it, say what you changed. Never blame a colleague; it always backfires.
Why are you leaving?Whether you are running away or moving toward somethingName what you want next, not what you are escaping. Stay positive about your current employer.
Where do you see yourself in five years?Whether your ambition fits their planDescribe growing within the kind of work they do, not a specific title you cannot promise.
What salary are you expecting?Whether you have researched the marketDefer if early: "I'd like to understand the full package first." See the salary negotiation guide.

4. By Format

  • Phone screen — Fifteen minutes, mostly qualification. Prepare a two-sentence pitch and three questions. Send a follow-up email.
  • Video call — Test the link, lighting and audio a day before. Look at the camera, not the screen. Put the interviewer name where you can see it. Silence for two seconds while thinking is fine; filler words are not.
  • In person — Arrive ten minutes early, bring two printed copies of your resume, and note the names of everyone you meet.
  • Panel — Answer the person who asked, then glance around. Different people probe different parts; do not assume a topic is finished.
  • Case or exercise — Clarify the problem and constraints out loud before starting. Thinking aloud scores higher than a silent perfect answer.
  • Technical — Explain your reasoning as you go. It is not a quiz; it is a check on how you would work with colleagues.
  • Final round with leadership — Usually about motivation and fit. Have a specific, researched reason for choosing this company over the others you are interviewing.

5. Questions To Ask Them

Always ask, even if you feel you already know. Two or three, asked near the end, signal interest and preparation.

  • "What does success look like in this role after the first six months?"
  • "Why is the position open, and what changed?"
  • "How does the team decide when to disagree, and how is that resolved?"
  • "What is the hardest part of this job right now?"
  • "What does the interview process look like from here, and on what timeline?"

Avoid, at least before you have an offer: holiday policy, sick leave, office perks, pension percentage, and "what's in it for me". Those are negotiation or HR-conversation topics.

6. Follow-Up That Works

Within 24 hours, under 120 words. Thank them by name, reference one specific thing from the conversation, restate why you want the role, and add anything you forgot to say properly — a relevant result, a clarification about a question. Nothing else.

A good one reads like this: "Thank you for the conversation on Tuesday. Your point about onboarding new analysts taking six weeks is exactly the problem I spent last year solving — I cut ours to two by rebuilding the checklist and pairing a new hire with a mentor for their first month. That is the work I want to keep doing, which is why the role stood out." One paragraph. Specific, short, no flattery.

7. After The Interview

  1. Send the follow-up the same day.
  2. Write down every question you were asked while it is fresh — each round often repeats, and you will be ready the second time.
  3. Note what to improve while honest feelings are fresh.
  4. Keep applying elsewhere. Waiting on one process is the most common way people stay stuck.
  5. If you get an offer, do not accept immediately. See salary negotiation — and note the deadline pressure the employer will apply.
If the interview invitation itself looks off — an unverifiable recruiter, a different company domain, or a request for payment — stop and read job search safety before replying. Verify the company independently before you hand over a document, join a link, or give a date.
