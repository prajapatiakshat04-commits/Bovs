Resumes & CVs — Write One That Actually Gets Read

A resume has one job: get you to the interview. Not to list everything you have ever done — to prove, in a few seconds of scanning, that you can do the job being offered. This guide covers structure, the achievement-bullet formula, ATS-safe formatting, and how to tailor without rewriting from scratch each time.

The single biggest upgrade: most resumes describe duties ("responsible for handling customer queries"). Hiring managers scan for results ("cut average response time from 9 hours to 2 by introducing a triage queue"). The second sentence gets you interviews.

1. How Long Should It Be?

Your situationTarget lengthWhy
Student, fresh graduate, career switcher1 pageLittle to show, so make every line count
Under 10 years experience1 pageEnough space for 4-5 strong roles
10+ years, or several employers2 pagesProgression and scope need room to show
Academic, research, legal, long publications2+ pages (use a CV)Conventional to include full detail
Senior leadership, board-level2-3 pagesScope of ownership is the evidence

A "CV" and a "resume" are the same document in practice — the difference is regional convention and expected depth. In the US and UK a resume is typically 1-2 pages and targeted; in much of Europe, Asia and academia, a CV is longer, chronological and complete. Sending the wrong format for your region reads as carelessness.

2. The Structure That Works

Use a single-column layout in this order. Every section is optional except contact details and experience — drop what you have nothing to show for.

  1. Contact header — Name, role title, email, phone, city, LinkedIn. One line each, no passport number, no date of birth, no marital status, no photo unless your region expects one.
  2. Professional summary — 2-3 lines: your role, your years, your strongest domain, and the kind of work you want next. This is a positioning statement, not a biography.
  3. Experience — Reverse chronological. Company, title, dates, then 3-5 bullets per role. This section carries the most weight, so it goes highest.
  4. Skills — Group by type: technical, tools, soft. Only list what you would happily be tested on.
  5. Projects — For students, career switchers, or anyone whose day job undersells their actual skills. Include a link if it is live.
  6. Education — Degree, institution, year. Add grades only if strong. Relevant certifications go here.
  7. Extras — Languages, volunteering, professional memberships, publications. Last, and only if relevant.

3. The Achievement-Bullet Formula

The most reliable structure is: action verb + what you did + the result, ideally with a number. Write the bullet, then ask "so what?" and add the outcome.

Instead ofWrite
Responsible for social media accountsGrew organic social reach from 4k to 31k in 14 months by shifting 60% of content to short-form video
Worked with the sales teamBuilt a lead-scoring sheet that helped sales prioritise 200+ monthly enquiries, lifting close rate from 18% to 27%
Helped with onboarding new hiresRewrote the onboarding checklist, cutting new-hire setup time from 5 days to 2
Handled customer complaintsResolved 40+ escalations a month while holding a 96% satisfaction score

Strong action verbs to open a bullet: built, launched, cut, grew, reduced, increased, automated, redesigned, migrated, negotiated, led, delivered, solved, saved, standardised, eliminated, recovered.

If you genuinely have no numbers, use scale, time or scope instead: "processed 300+ tickets a week", "managed a 12-person team", "delivered 3 launches in one quarter". Never invent a figure — an interview will test it.

4. ATS-Safe Formatting

Most medium and large employers screen through an applicant tracking system first. It does not "reject" you; it parses your file into fields, and whatever it cannot read never reaches the recruiter. Design for the parser.

DoAvoid
Single column, top to bottomTwo-column or sidebar designs
Standard section headings: Experience, Education, SkillsCreative headings a parser does not recognise
Real text, not images of textCharts, graphs and text boxes
Standard fonts: Arial, Calibri, Georgia, TimesExotic fonts, or text smaller than 10pt
Consistent date format (Jan 2023 – Present)Header/footer content with contact details
Bullet characters are plain hyphens or standard bulletsSymbols, dingbats, emoji as section markers
PDF for most roles, DOCX where the portal demands itSaving as .pages or .key from a Mac
Keywords help, stuffing does not. Mirror the exact wording from the job posting for skills you genuinely have — "SQL" not "database querying", "Agile/Scrum" not "fast-paced team player". A keyword-matching system rewards the literal phrase; a human reader punishes obvious padding.

5. Tailoring Without Rewriting

Do not write a fresh resume for every application. Keep a master version, then for each role spend twenty minutes on three things.

  1. Reorder — Put the two or three experiences most relevant to this posting first, even if they are not your most recent. Chronology is a default, not a law.
  2. Rewrite the summary — Two lines naming the role and the problem this company has.
  3. Swap three bullets — Choose bullets that mirror the job's actual responsibilities and keywords.

If a posting asks for a cover letter, write it fresh but short: three paragraphs — why this role, what in your history proves you can do it, and one specific reason this company. A generic letter is worse than none, because it signals you mass-applied.

6. Common Mistakes That Get You Filtered

  • Duty lists instead of achievements. Nobody progresses on "responsible for".
  • Undated or unquantified everything. No way to judge growth.
  • Typos in the first line. Recruiters often stop there.
  • Objective statement ("seeking a challenging role"). Outdated and self-focused; the summary replaced it.
  • Listing every technology you have ever opened. Dilutes the three you are actually good at.
  • Full home address, DOB, marital status, nationality. Unnecessary, and risky in some jurisdictions.
  • Same file name forever. Use Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf — some systems show only the file name.
  • Sending the master version unedited. Obvious in the summary, fatal at the shortlist stage.

7. The 20-Minute Self-Audit

Print it. Read only the first six seconds' worth — top third of page one. Ask a friend to do the same and say what your job title is. If they cannot say it in one sentence, the top of your resume is not doing its job. Then read every bullet and ask "so what?" three times. That single exercise removes more weak lines than any template will add.

Keep a PDF and a DOCX of the final version. Send the PDF by email when you can. Upload the DOCX only when a portal explicitly requests it. Open the file once more before sending — fonts shift and columns move when a document is saved on a different machine.
