Career Growth — Getting Ahead Without Waiting To Be Told

Promotion is decided on evidence, not potential. Most people waiting to be promoted have not yet done the next job — they are still doing the current one well. This guide covers building a promotion case in writing, finding stretch opportunities, developing specific skills, recognising a plateau, and knowing when to stay and when to move.

The mindset shift: stop asking "when will I be promoted" and start asking "what does the role above mine actually require, and which of those am I not yet doing?" That single question is the entire strategy.

1. Find Out What The Next Level Requires

You are guessing. Fix that in one conversation with your manager.

  • Ask for the actual expectations of the role above yours, in writing if you can get them.
  • Ask what separates people at your level from people at the next one.
  • Ask where you currently sit against those expectations, and what the biggest gap is.
  • Ask for one specific behaviour you could change in the next quarter.

Then write the answer down and set a date to revisit it. This is the highest-return conversation available to you, and almost nobody has it.

2. Build The Evidence File

When a promotion is discussed, the manager who argues for you is usually working from memory. Give them material.

LevelWhat it usually means
Individual contributorYou do your own work well and independently
Senior ICYou handle ambiguity, unblock others, and own outcomes beyond your tasks
Lead / ManagerYou now succeed through other people — hiring, feedback, prioritisation
Senior / HeadYou set direction, manage stakeholders, and handle what nobody has a template for

Keep a running document of outcomes: projects delivered, numbers improved, problems solved, people you mentored or hired, times you handled a crisis well. One line each, dated, with a number where possible. Review it before any review cycle, any interview, and any time you think about leaving.

3. Take On Scope That Is Genuinely Harder

  • Volunteer for the project nobody wants. The ugly, underspecified, cross-team problem is where you become the person people remember.
  • Own something end to end — not a slice of it. Start to finish, including the parts that are not your strength.
  • Fix something broken. Everyone enjoys new work; nobody volunteers for the mess. That is why it is an opportunity.
  • Mentor someone. It develops communication and judgement, and it is visible.
  • Represent the team externally. A client call, a conference, a cross-company meeting.
  • Take the role before you get it. Doing the work is what makes the promotion conversation short.
Careful with "visibility".b> Working on visible things is not the same as doing visible work. Announcing busywork reads worse than quietly solving a hard problem. Do the hard thing, then make sure it is written down.

4. Develop Specific Skills, Not Random Ones

If your gap isThen
Technical depthGo deep on one system or tool until you could teach it. Certification helps only if the job genuinely requires one.
CommunicationWrite. Publish a short piece monthly. Presenting to your team is the cheapest possible practice.
LeadershipLead a project, run retrospectives, and give feedback you have been avoiding.
Business judgementAsk "why are we doing this" about every decision, and spend time with customers or clients.
VisibilityPresent your work quarterly to a real audience, even if it is small.

Pick one gap and a ninety-day plan. Five gaps and no progress is the common failure.

5. Recognising A Plateau

Sometimes you are ready and the company has nowhere to promote you. The signs:

  • The role above you has been open, or filled by someone external, twice.
  • You have asked twice what growth looks like and got a vague answer both times.
  • You are doing the work of two people with the title of one, indefinitely.
  • New responsibility arrives without new pay or title, and this has become normal.
  • Your network is growing faster than your role.

Do not fix this by waiting harder. Ask once, directly, for a timeframe. If the honest answer is "there is nothing here for the next year or two", that is information — not a verdict on your ability. Decide deliberately: stay and build something specific (a team, a product, a specialism), or move to a market with the roles you want.

6. Staying Or Moving

Stay ifMove if
You are still learning something that mattersYou have learned what you came for
The team is good and you like the peopleThe role has shrunk or changed for two years
You have a specific thing you want to build hereGrowth requires a company size or stage you will not reach
Your manager invests in youFeedback has stopped entirely
Mobility is slow but realYou are comfortable, and comfortable is the problem

Judged by capability gained rather than frequency, around two to three years in a role is usually enough. Moving too fast means never learning anything deeply and never building a track record. Move when you are ready — see salary negotiation for the offer conversation, and interviews for the process itself.

7. Keep Your Career Optional

  • Keep a current CV. An updated one turns an offer into a choice instead of a rescue.
  • Keep visible skills. Your profile, your writing, your public work.
  • Keep your network warm. See networking.
  • Keep earning. Money buys the ability to refuse a bad offer without panic.
  • Know your notice period and your visa status. Both are constraints, and both are easier to plan around now than in a panic.
A career is a project you are the lead on. Nobody is coming to promote you as a favour. Decide deliberately what you want to be doing in two years, what skills that requires, what evidence proves you are already doing it, and who needs to know. Then have those conversations, in writing, before the review cycle.
